 1. Matrix systems, row reduction, and inverses

 Reduced row echelon form
 A matrix is in reduced row echelon form (RREF) when:
●   Every nonzero row has a leading 1.
●   Each leading 1 is the only nonzero entry in its column.
●   Leading 1s move strictly to the right as you move downward.
●   All zero rows are below the nonzero rows.

 The first sample test asks you to recognize these rules across several example matrices. [Math, LAs26t1sample.pdf,
 Page 7]


 Solving a system by row reduction
 Write the system as an augmented matrix, perform legal row operations, and interpret the result:
●   A pivot in every variable column means there are no free variables.
●   A free variable produces a family of solutions.
●   A row of the form 0 = nonzero means no solution.

 The sample includes one homogeneous system whose only solution is the zero vector, one nonhomogeneous system
 with solution [a,0,1-a]^T, and one homogeneous system with solution [0,-a,a]^T. [Math, LAs26t1sample.pdf, Page 4]

    2. Vector spaces, bases, and linear maps

    Fast subspace test
    For a subset W of a known vector space, check:
1      The zero vector is in W.
2      W is closed under vector addition.
3      W is closed under scalar multiplication.

    A line through the origin, such as y=3x, is a subspace of R2. A shifted line, such as y=x+3, is not because it misses the
    zero vector. Sets restricted to polynomials of exactly one degree or degree at least a threshold also fail because they
    omit the zero polynomial and may fail closure through cancellation. [Math, LAs26t2sample-1.pdf, Page 1]

    4. Exam workflow and common mistakes

    A reliable order of attack
1      Write matrix dimensions before multiplying.
2      For systems, row reduce carefully and mark pivot and free variables.
3      For subspaces, test the zero vector first; it often gives the fastest counterexample.
4      For bases and independence, turn the vectors into matrix columns.
5      For null space, parameterize the free variables and extract basis vectors.
6      For column space, use pivot positions from RREF but columns from the original matrix.
7      For eigenvalues, compute and factor the characteristic polynomial before solving eigenspaces.
8      For diagonalization, match the order of eigenvector columns in P with eigenvalues in D.

    These methods reflect the recurring task formats across the three sample packets. [Math, LAs26t1sample.pdf, Pages
    3-9] [Math, LAs26t2sample-1.pdf, Pages 1-9] [Math, LSa26t3sample-1.pdf, Pages 2-8]


    Frequent traps
●      Multiplying matrices whose inner dimensions do not match. [Math, LAs26t1sample.pdf, Pages 8-9]
●      Assuming a shifted line or fixed-coordinate set is a subspace. [Math, LAs26t2sample-1.pdf, Pages 1-2]
●      Taking column-space basis vectors from the RREF instead of the original matrix. [Math, LAs26t2sample-1.pdf, Page 8]
●      Forgetting that a repeated eigenvalue may have too few eigenvectors. [Math, LSa26t3sample-1.pdf, Page 8]
●      Forgetting that eigenvectors may be rescaled: any nonzero scalar multiple is also an eigenvector. [Math,
       LSa26t3sample-1.pdf, Pages 3-4]
